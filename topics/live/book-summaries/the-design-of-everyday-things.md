# The Design of Everyday Things

Don Norman. Good design isn't taste. It's making objects that tell you how to use them, so the failure is the design's fault, not users'.

## Affordance

The relationship between an object and a user: what actions the object makes possible. A flat plate affords pushing. A handle affords pulling. The affordance is in the pairing, not the object alone.

A door with a flat metal plate is asking to be pushed. A door with a handle is asking to be pulled. When they're wired backward, you fight the door every time.

## Signifier

The perceivable signal that tells you where and how to act. Affordances can exist and stay invisible. A signifier makes them visible. A "push" label, an arrow, the worn spot where a thousand hands have already pressed.

A glass door with no handle and no plate affords both push and pull, so you guess wrong. Add a "PUSH" sign and the guessing stops. The signifier does the work the affordance couldn't.

## Conceptual model

The simplified story you build of how a thing works. It doesn't have to be complete or accurate. It has to be good enough to predict what happens and guide your next move. A car's conceptual model is that you turn the key to start, push the gas to go, and push the brake to stop. 

## Mapping

The relationship between a control and its effect. Good mapping borrows from space and nature so the link is obvious without a manual.

Stove burner knobs laid out in the same pattern as the burners: you know which knob lights which ring without looking. A car seat adjuster shaped like the seat, where you push the front edge up to raise the front. The control looks like the thing it moves.
