# Chapter 3: Knowledge in the Head and in the World

You don't need precise knowledge to act precisely. Knowledge lives half in your head and half in the world, and design decides how much you're forced to memorize.

## Precise behavior from imprecise knowledge

Four reasons sloppy knowledge is enough:

1. Knowledge sits in the world too. You read what you need off the object at the moment you need it.
2. Precision isn't required. You only need enough to pick the right option among the alternatives in front of you.
3. Natural constraints. Physics rules out most wrong moves before you make them.
4. Cultural constraints and conventions. Red means stop; learned once, reused everywhere.

The penny: almost nobody can recall which way Lincoln faces, yet nobody pays with the wrong coin. You only need enough to tell a penny from the other coins in circulation. Anything past that is wasted memory.

## Memory: knowledge in the head

- Short-term (working) memory: tiny, about five items, wiped by any distraction. Never make a user hold a code in their head while doing something else with it.
- Long-term memory: huge but slow to write into, and retrieval is reconstruction, not playback. Meaningful material sticks; arbitrary material doesn't.
- Three ways people remember how things work: rote memorization (arbitrary, fragile, no recovery when it fails), memory backed by constraints and meaning, and mental models (forget the fact and you re-derive it from the model).
- Approximate models beat exact ones for daily use. Pi as "about 3" gets most estimates done. Conscious thinking is slow and expensive, so we trade precision for speed.

## Prospective memory: remembering to remember

Remembering to do something later needs two parts: the signal (the reminder that something is due) and the message (what that something is). A string around your finger is signal without message. A note in a drawer is message without signal. A good reminder puts both in the world at the time and place you'll need them, which is why the calendar notification beats both.

## The tradeoff

Knowledge in the world: always available, nothing to learn, but it clutters the design and reading it off the environment is slower.

Knowledge in the head: fast and clean once learned, allows minimal design, but learning costs time and memory fails.

Good design pushes as much as it can into the world without drowning the user in clutter, and lets experts graduate to head knowledge for speed.

## Natural mapping levels

Best: controls mounted right on the thing being controlled.
Second: controls close to the thing.
Third: controls laid out in the same spatial pattern as the things they control (stove knobs matching the burners).
Anything worse needs labels, and a label is an admission the mapping failed.

## Culture decides what "natural" means

When you scroll, does the text move or the window move? Both models are internally consistent. Touchscreens flipped the convention and desktops followed. Mappings feel natural because a culture drilled them in, not because they're universal. Design for the convention your users already carry.
