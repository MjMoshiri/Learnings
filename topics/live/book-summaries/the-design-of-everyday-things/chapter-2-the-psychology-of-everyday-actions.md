# Chapter 2: The Psychology of Everyday Actions

People build a story of how a thing works and act on it. When the thing behaves differently than the story predicts, that gap is a design problem, not a user failure. Good design closes the distance between what you want to do and what the object does.

## The seven stages of action

The model of how you do anything. One goal, an execution side, an evaluation side.

1. Goal: decide what you want.
2. Plan the action.
3. Specify the action sequence.
4. Perform it.
5. Perceive the state of the world.
6. Interpret what you perceived.
7. Compare the outcome against the goal.

Stages 2 to 4 are execution, the doing. Stages 5 to 7 are evaluation, the checking. The seven stages double as a design checklist: for each one, ask whether the user can tell what to do and whether they can tell it worked.

## The two gulfs

The two ideas to keep from this chapter.

- Gulf of execution: the gap between your intention and what the device lets you do. "How do I work this?" Close it by making actions and controls obvious.
- Gulf of evaluation: the gap between the device's state and your ability to read it. "What happened, did it work?" Close it with clear feedback.

## Three levels of processing

Norman ties action to how the brain handles experience at three levels.

- Visceral: fast, automatic, gut reaction. How something looks and feels before you think.
- Behavioral: learned routines and the feel of use. Where feedback and expectation live.
- Reflective: conscious thought, meaning, the story you tell afterward.

Visceral and behavioral drive the lower stages, perform and perceive. Reflective drives goals and the final interpretation.

## People as storytellers

We invent causal explanations to make sense of things, even when the story is wrong. The mind wants a coherent model. Design should hand you an accurate one instead of leaving you to guess.

## Blaming the wrong thing

When a badly designed object fails them, people blame themselves. Norman says that's backward.

- Learned helplessness: repeated failure convinces people they can't do something ("I'm bad at tech"), when the fault is the design.
- Read errors as a signal the design needs fixing, not as user stupidity. Blame the design, not the person.

## What to keep

- When something is hard to use, suspect the design first.
- Good design gives you discoverability (you can tell what's possible) and feedback (you can tell what happened).
- Walk the seven stages as a checklist when you build something.
