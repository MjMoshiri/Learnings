# Chapter 7: Design in the World of Business

Good design loses to bad design in the market all the time, and it's not because users are irrational. It's because the forces that decide what ships aren't optimizing for the thing designers care about.

## Incremental vs. radical innovation

Almost all innovation is incremental: small, continuous improvements to an existing product line. Radical innovation, a genuinely new technology or way of doing things, is rare and mostly comes from outside the companies with the most to lose from it. Human needs and abilities change on an evolutionary timescale; technology changes every product cycle. Good design has to bridge that gap, building interfaces that fit stable human psychology on top of whatever the current technology happens to be.

## Why worse designs win

Feature checklists sell. In a showroom or a spec sheet comparison, a product with more features looks like the better product, even if most of those features go unused and their presence makes the whole thing harder to use. This is creeping featurism: each individual feature is easy to justify on its own, engineering adds it, and the sum is a bloated, confusing product that nobody asked for as a whole. Companies keep adding because the alternative, saying no to a feature that some customer segment wants, feels like leaving revenue on the table.

## Standardization and lock-in

Once a design becomes the standard, being better isn't enough to replace it. QWERTY is the standard example: objectively worse than alternative keyboard layouts for typing speed, and untouchable because everyone already learned it and every keyboard already has it. Switching costs protect the incumbent regardless of merit. Real change in a locked-in standard usually needs either a new market with no installed base, or a shift large enough that the old switching cost stops mattering.

## Design has a weak seat at the table

Design competes with engineering, marketing, sales, and finance for what a product becomes, and it tends to lose. Cost and schedule are concrete numbers executives can act on. Usability is a judgment call that's hard to quantify until the product ships and support tickets show up. Companies optimizing for the next quarterly release can't easily justify the kind of fundamental rethinking good design sometimes needs, since it doesn't pay off inside that cycle.

## What to keep

- A feature that's easy to justify in isolation can still make the product worse as a whole. Justify features against the full product, not just their own case.
- Don't expect the better design to win by default. It has to overcome switching costs, sales incentives, and short release cycles too.
- When usability loses an internal argument, it's usually not because it's wrong. It's because it's the only side without a number attached to it.
