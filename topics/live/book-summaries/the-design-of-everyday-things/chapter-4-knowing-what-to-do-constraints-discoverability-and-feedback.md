# Chapter 4: Knowing What to Do: Constraints, Discoverability, and Feedback

How do you operate something you've never seen before? Constraints cut the options until the right action is the obvious one, and feedback confirms it worked. Discoverability tells you what you can do; feedback tells you what just happened.

## The Lego motorcycle

Norman gave adults a 13-piece Lego motorcycle and no instructions. They built it correctly anyway. The pieces carried the knowledge: every part had only a few sensible placements, so the constraints did the work of a manual.

## Four kinds of constraints

- Physical: pegs and holes fit only one way. Strongest when visible before you act, so you never attempt the wrong move instead of discovering the failure mid-action. A key that works in either orientation is a physical constraint removed on purpose.
- Cultural: the rider faces forward, the red light goes on the back, because the culture says rear lights are red. No physics, pure convention.
- Semantic: the windshield protects the rider's face, so it sits in front of the rider. The meaning of the situation fixes the placement.
- Logical: one piece left, one spot left, so that's where it goes. Natural mappings are logical constraints: the spatial layout of controls tells you which control does what.

## Everyday applications

- Doors: the hardware should announce push or pull. A flat plate says push, a handle says pull. If a door needs a sign, the design already failed.
- Light switches: nobody knows which switch runs which light because a one-dimensional row maps onto a two-dimensional room. Norman's fix: mount the switches on a floor plan of the room, one switch at each light's position.
- Faucets: two things you care about (temperature and flow) run by two knobs that each mix into both. Which knob is hot, which way is more? Convention answers it, and breaking the convention scalds people.

## Forcing functions

Constraints strong enough to physically block the error:

- Interlock: forces operations into sequence. The microwave cuts power the instant the door opens.
- Lock-in: keeps an operation from ending too early. The "save before quitting?" dialog.
- Lockout: keeps you out of a dangerous place. A gate at the ground floor of a stairwell, so people fleeing a fire don't keep running into the basement.

## Conventions

Conventions are cultural constraints that harden over time. Once one is established, breaking it for a small gain is a net loss: if the new way is only slightly better, staying consistent wins. Destination-control elevators (pick your floor in the lobby, no buttons in the cab) are genuinely more efficient and still confuse everyone, because they break a convention a century old. Hot on the left, cold on the right; deviate and users get hurt.

## Sound as signifier

When the eyes are busy, sound carries the feedback: the kettle whistle, the solid click of a door latch catching. Electric cars are dangerously silent, so regulators make them add sound back on purpose. But sound is blunt: it annoys, it distracts, and you can't point your ears away. Use it when nothing else reaches the user.
