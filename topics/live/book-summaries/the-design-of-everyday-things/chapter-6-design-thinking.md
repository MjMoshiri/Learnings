# Chapter 6: Design Thinking

Most failed designs solve the wrong problem well. The fix isn't a better solution process, it's spending more time figuring out what the actual problem is before touching solutions at all.

## The double diamond

Design has two divergence-then-convergence cycles, not one:

- Diamond 1: figure out the right problem. Diverge by exploring the space widely, converge on a specification of what's actually being solved.
- Diamond 2: figure out the right solution. Diverge by generating many candidate solutions, converge on the one to build.

The common failure is skipping diamond 1 entirely and jumping straight into diamond 2 on whatever problem was handed to you. You end up with a well-executed answer to the wrong question.

## Human-centered design, four activities on a loop

1. Observation: watch real people in their actual context, not in a conference room describing what they think they do.
2. Idea generation: many candidate solutions, deliberately not converging early.
3. Prototyping: quick, cheap, disposable versions built to test one question, not to be shipped.
4. Testing: with real users, on the actual prototype, not a description of it.

These aren't sequential steps you complete once. They loop, and any stage can send you back to an earlier one when it reveals the problem was misunderstood.

## Don't ask people what they want

Interviews and surveys are weak tools for design research because people are bad at reporting their own behavior and needs. They rationalize after the fact, answer the question they think you want, and don't have introspective access to a lot of what drives their actions. Watching what people actually do, in the place they do it, surfaces problems nobody would think to mention.

## Design problems are wicked

A wicked problem has no definitive formulation and no stopping rule. There's no version of "we found the correct answer," because attempting a solution changes your understanding of the problem itself. This is why fixed up-front specifications don't work for design: you don't know enough yet to write them, and by the time you do, you've already learned things that would change them.

## What to keep

- Before proposing a fix, ask whether the stated problem is the real one. Most bad designs answer the wrong question well.
- Watch behavior instead of asking about it. What people say they do and what they do diverge more than you'd expect.
- Cheap prototypes early beat one polished attempt late. The point of a prototype is to be wrong quickly.
- Treat requirements as something that emerges through the process, not something you lock down before starting.
