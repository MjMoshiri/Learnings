# The gist

- Three mutation types: point, deletion, insertion. Indels frameshift everything downstream. Point mutations that leave a protein working slightly better or worse are the molecular engine of gradualism.
- Single genes reach into behavior: a calcium channel mutation raises autism risk, a dopamine transporter mutation gives mice anhedonia, D4 insertions raise ADHD risk, vasopressin receptor insertions shift pair-bond fidelity.
- Leptin receptor deletions run in families as generations of morbid obesity. The brain never gets the satiation signal, and no amount of discipline fixes a missing receptor. No free will, in one gene.
- FOXP2, the language gene, is hyper-conserved: 2 amino acids separate us from chimps, ~1.5 from Neanderthals. A family with a broken copy had deficits in production and comprehension both.
- Codon redundancy hands the genome a built-in null hypothesis. 64 codons for ~20 amino acids, so a predictable fraction of point mutations are silent. Fewer silent changes than chance predicts means positive selection, the variant swept the population. A functional variant you never see at all means negative selection, its carriers are gone.
- FOXP2 was called the strongest case of positive selection in the human genome, fixed within the last 100k years. Skewed sample. The selection ran ~250k years ago, before the Neanderthal split, so Neanderthals had our language gene. Of the ~4% Neanderthal DNA in Europeans, negative selection purged most; the survivors code for red hair, pale skin, freckles.
- Genes don't know what they're doing. A gene deciding when to make its protein is a recipe deciding when the cake gets baked.
- 98% of human DNA is non-coding, the old junk DNA. It's the instruction manual: promoters and switches upstream of genes, flipped when a transcription factor docks in. 2% recipes, the rest about when to cook.
- Transcription factors don't decide either. Environment does: testosterone binds its receptor and the complex becomes the transcription factor for muscle genes, the smell of your newborn turns on hypothalamic oxytocin genes. If-then clauses all the way down.
- Regulation beats genes quantitatively. 70% of brain-expressed genes are brain-only, region- and stage-specific. Bigger genomes spend a bigger share on regulation. We share 98.5% of our DNA with chimps and the differences sit disproportionately in transcription-factor genes. Wilson's theory: evolution edits the switches.
- Epigenetics: environment setting gene regulation for decades, no sequence change. Chromatin locked open or closed over a gene, methyl caps on promoters. Marks can be lifelong but they're reversible; the right later environment, good therapy after a bad childhood, takes them off.
- Meaney's rats: attentive mothers epigenetically open the glucocorticoid receptor gene in the pup's brain. The pup grows into a calm adult, mothers attentively herself, and reprints the mark on her own pups. Suomi: mothering style marks ~4,000 genes in monkey infants.
- The Maasai version: undernourished girls grow narrow pelvises, then eat little while pregnant so the fetus fits through delivery. The daughter grows a narrow pelvis too. No DNA changed.
- Route two skips behavior. Some marks survive the gamete wipe, and proteins in sperm cytosol re-silence the same gene after fertilization. Stressed father rats produce stress-prone offspring; strip the protein from the sperm and it stops. Acquired traits inherited with the sequence untouched, Neo-Lamarckian.

## Makes you think

The fact that 98% of it is instructions and the fact that, despite how much we do want to claim that these are genetics, environment actually do have an impact. Most probably, this also means that perspective and how you want to do your life, and the time that you would put into therapy, self-improvement, and growth also have a big impact. Maybe we are not as doomed as we thought we are.
