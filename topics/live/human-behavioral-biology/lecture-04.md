# The gist

- Lecture 2's kin selection assumed organisms just know who their relatives are. Lecture 4 is about the actual mechanisms behind that assumption.
- Kin selection and inbreeding avoidance pull in opposite directions: cooperate/mate with relatives vs. don't mate with relatives (recessive mutations go homozygous and get expressed). Theoretical models balancing the two predict the sweet spot is third cousins.
- That prediction holds up. Birds seek out third cousins for mating even after dispersing, and third-cousin offspring survive best. Iceland's genealogical records show the same thing in humans historically: third-cousin marriages had the best reproductive outcomes.
- Cheney and Seyfarth's baboon playback experiments: baboons only react strongly to a "dominance reversal" (a low-rank animal sounding dominant over a high-rank one) when it crosses family lines. They're tracking kinship and rank as separate variables, even for a neighboring troop.
- Kin recognition runs on a hierarchy of mechanisms, from dumb to smart:
  - Cellular level: surface proteins whose only job is identity, like-binds-to-like. Sperm cells cooperate and swim faster alongside sperm from the same man or a full brother, less with distant kin. Graded binding affinity signals degree of relatedness.
  - It's innate, not learned. Full siblings raised apart, who've never met, still recognize each other and treat each other differently than strangers, scaled down for half-siblings. Runs on pheromones, not visual learning.
  - The pheromone signal piggybacks on the immune system. MHC genes generate a self-marker unique to each individual, and that same protein goes airborne, ending up in saliva and armpits as the "smell."
  - Oxytocin and vasopressin gate the social meaning of smell. Knock out their receptors in the olfactory system and the animal can still smell fine and learn odor discriminations, but goes socially anosmic: it can't tell individuals or relatedness apart by smell anymore.
  - Adult neurogenesis (dogma-denied for decades, career-destroying for Joseph Altman when he first reported it) turns out real in the olfactory bulb. Rising prolactin during pregnancy rebuilds a mother rat's smell-processing system right before birth so she can imprint on her newborn. Block the prolactin receptor there and she goes anosmic to her own pups. Probably a spandrel explanation for pregnancy smell/food aversions, no toxin-avoidance story needed.
  - Imprinting can run on acquired scent tags instead of genetic ones. Smear another mother's amniotic fluid on a washed newborn goat and the other mother accepts it as hers.
  - Real cognitive strategies show up through mistakes: a baboon mid-kidnap grabs the wrong infant, catches himself, drops it. Sheep splotched with matching blue paint start acting affiliative toward each other, an induced green-beard effect. Male baboons defend the offspring of females they mated with at peak fertility, and they're statistically accurate about it (confirmed by DNA), but it's probabilistic cognition, not certainty: a male who mated with two females at peak cycle defended only his actual kid when their offspring fought. Sunfish do the probabilistic version too: a male who just watches his mate placed near another male, with no chance they actually mated, still cuts back his paternal investment afterward.
- In humans: fusiform cortex specializes for whatever category matters to you (faces for most people), and about 75% of people show weaker fusiform response to other-race faces. Fetal learning is real too: babies whose mothers read "The Cat in the Hat" aloud daily in the last trimester orient toward that story after birth, and specifically toward their mother's voice reading it, not their father's.
- Underneath conscious recognition, humans also run unconscious rules that don't track real genetics. The Westermarck effect: kids raised in close proximity for their first ~6 years essentially never marry each other as adults, zero genetic relatedness or not (Israeli kibbutz peer groups, Taiwanese minor-marriage custom).
- Infant kinship heuristic: sharing food, utensils, or saliva reads as a relatedness signal. 5-7 year olds infer sibling vs. friend by who shares a straw with whom. 12-month-olds in puppet studies favor the puppet that "shared saliva" with them.
- Because human kin recognition runs on inference, culture, and unconscious heuristics instead of a hardwired readout, humans are unusually easy to manipulate about who counts as kin, in both directions. Pseudo-kinship: basic training manufactures "band of brothers" loyalty strong enough that a German-American and Italian-American soldier would risk their lives for each other over a blood-cousin on the enemy side. Post-revolutionary societies do the same with "brother"/"comrade," like the French Revolution mandating "tu" for everyone.
- Pseudo-speciation is the dark mirror: making an actual human group feel alien enough that killing them doesn't register as killing a human. Sapolsky's live example: Netanyahu invoking "the Amalekites," the one tribe the Old Testament commands totally exterminated, in a speech about Gaza. Historians find little evidence Amalekites existed as a real tribe. Some right-wing Orthodox scholars have mapped Palestinians onto them directly; others call it metaphor. Same recognition machinery as the rest of the lecture, running in reverse.

## Makes you think

Line up the mechanisms and you get a stack: surface proteins, then pheromones, then hormone-gated smell, then acquired scent tags, then actual inference that reveals itself through mistakes. Each layer up is less hardwired and more computed. Sperm can't be wrong about who they're next to. A baboon grabbing the wrong infant can be, and the mistake is the proof that something is being calculated rather than just triggered.

The last layer, the one that runs on story and culture instead of a direct signal, is exactly the layer that can be hijacked in both directions. Basic training turns strangers into brothers. A speech invoking a tribe historians doubt ever existed turns neighbors into something less than human. Same machinery, same "is this one of us" question, running from bacteria up to a prime minister's speech about Gaza six months before this lecture was given. That's not a metaphor about biology being misused. It's the actual circuitry doing what it's built to do, pointed wherever the story tells it to point.
