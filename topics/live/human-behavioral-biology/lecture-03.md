# The gist

- Lecture 3 finishes the evolution arc. Same tools as before (individual, kin, reciprocal), now pointed at competition between the sexes and at the cracks in the whole adaptationist story.
- Males and females play different games because the cheap cell and the expensive cell set different incentives. Sperm is cheap. Eggs and gestation are not. That asymmetry drives most of what follows.
- Sexual dimorphism splits animals into two stable species types. Dimorphic (tournament) species: sexes look very different, males much bigger, males fight hard for access, little paternal care, a few males father most of the offspring, low paternity certainty. Monomorphic (pair-bonding) species: sexes look alike, closer to monogamy, males invest in the young, higher paternity certainty.
- Humans sit awkwardly between the two. Some of our biology reads tournament, some reads pair-bonding. We are not a clean case, and that confusion shows up all over human mating.
- Sexual selection explains costly showy traits. A peacock's tail is a handicap. Surviving in spite of the cost is the honest signal, and it can run away from plain survival value.
- Competitive infanticide is a male reproductive strategy, not a glitch. A new male langur kills the nursing infants so the mothers cycle back into fertility sooner. Females push back: pseudo-estrus, blurring paternity, ending a pregnancy after a takeover.
- When males can't settle it by fighting, the competition moves to sperm. Relative testes size across species tracks how much sperm competition there is.
- Then Sapolsky spends the back half attacking the just-so-story habit, the reflex of inventing an adaptive reason for every trait.
- Not every trait is an adaptation. Gould and Lewontin's spandrels: some features are structural byproducts of building something else, carried along for free.
- Evolution may not be smooth. Punctuated equilibrium says long flat stretches get broken by fast bursts of change, often after something shakes a small population loose.
- Founder effects and drift move genes around with no selection story at all. Small isolated populations can fix a trait by luck.

## Makes you think

The first half is the sociobiology machine at full speed: name a behavior and it hands you the gene-level payoff. Sex differences, showy signals, even infanticide all fall out of one accounting rule about who pays for reproduction. It's powerful, and it's a little too easy.

The second half is Sapolsky turning the machine on itself. The trouble with adaptive reasoning is that it never fails. Any trait, real or made up, gets a story. Spandrels, drift, founder effects, and punctuated bursts are the reminder that "it exists, so it was selected for" isn't an argument. Some of biology is byproduct. Some is luck. The honest version keeps the selfish-gene logic but stops short of explaining everything with it.
